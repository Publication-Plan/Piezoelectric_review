    \begin{abstract}
        \noindent This research presents a comprehensive analysis of piezoelectric technology for energy harvesting, culminating in the design of a self-sufficient power system for oceanographic buoys. The study begins with the fundamental principles of piezoelectricity, examining material properties and the dual direct/inverse effect. It then explores energy harvesting configurations, analyzing their advantages and intrinsic limitations. Successful real-world implementations in infrastructure, wearables, and biomedical devices are reviewed. Finally, an integrated prototype is proposed: a pressurized module containing 24 PZT-5A discs, activated by wave-induced lever motion, coupled with an LTC3588-1 conditioning circuit and supercapacitor storage. Energy budget analysis confirms a substantial surplus (∼1400 mAh/day generated vs. 2.16 mAh/day consumed), validating the concept of perpetual autonomy for remote sensors. This work synthesizes theoretical analysis with practical engineering to address the critical challenge of powering offshore monitoring systems.
    \end{abstract}
    
    \vfill
    \noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Piezoelectric Energy Harvesting, PZT, Autonomous Sensors, Oceanographic Buoy, Power Management, Sustainable Monitoring.
\end{titlepage}

\section*{Introduction}

Piezoelectricity, discovered in 1880 by the Curie brothers, represents a cornerstone of modern transducer technology. Its ability to directly convert mechanical stress into electrical charge (direct effect) and vice versa (inverse effect) enables a wide range of applications, from precision sensors and actuators to emerging energy harvesting systems \cite{safari2008, jaffe1971}.

In the context of sustainable engineering and the Internet of Things (IoT), the search for autonomous power sources for remote sensors has become critical. Traditional solutions based on batteries or wired power present limitations in maintenance, environmental impact, and deployment flexibility. Piezoelectric energy harvesting offers a promising alternative by scavenging ambient mechanical energy from vibrations, impacts, or deformations.

This research systematically analyzes piezoelectric technology, from its basic principles to practical implementation. It aims to bridge theoretical knowledge with engineering design, culminating in a specific proposal for an oceanographic monitoring buoy. This buoy, part of the ARES collaboration, requires a reliable, maintenance-free power system to sustain its sensors (pH, conductivity, ORP, temperature) and LoRa communication module in the harsh marine environment.

By examining material properties, system design trade-offs, and successful case studies, this work establishes the framework for a viable self-powered solution. The final prototype design demonstrates how the intrinsic limitations of piezoelectricity can be overcome through careful mechanical and electronic engineering to achieve true energy autonomy.

% ==================== PUNTO 1: PIEZOELÉCTRICOS. CONCEPTOS BÁSICOS ====================
\section*{1. Piezoelectric Fundamentals}

\subsection*{1.1 Definition and Dual Principle}
Piezoelectricity is the property of certain materials (piezoelectrics) to generate an electric potential difference (\textbf{direct effect}) when subjected to mechanical deformation, such as pressure or vibration. This phenomenon is based on the displacement of charge centers within the material's crystalline structure. Conversely, these materials undergo mechanical deformation when subjected to an external electric field (\textbf{inverse effect}). This duality allows them to function as both sensors (converting force into electrical signal) and actuators (converting electricity into motion) \cite{safari2008}.

\subsection*{1.2 Brief Historical Context}
The effect was discovered and characterized experimentally in 1880 by Pierre and Jacques Curie, using crystals such as quartz, tourmaline, and topaz. Their work established the quantitative relationship between applied pressure and generated charge, laying the theoretical and practical foundations for the development of this technology \cite{jaffe1971}.

\subsection*{1.3 Types of Materials}
Piezoelectric materials are classified as natural and synthetic, each with distinctive characteristics:

\noindent\textbf{Natural:} Quartz is the most representative, valued for its stability and precision in sensors and oscillators, although its energy performance is modest.

\noindent\textbf{Synthetic:} PZT (Lead Zirconate Titanate) ceramics stand out, widely used for their high piezoelectric coefficients and sensitivity, ideal for transducers and energy harvesting. Polymers like PVDF offer flexibility for applications in wearables or irregular surfaces. Recently, lead-free materials like KNN (Potassium Sodium Niobate) have gained relevance for sustainability \cite{safari2008, uchino2010}.

\subsection*{1.4 Key Properties}
The performance of a piezoelectric material is governed by specific properties:

\noindent\textbf{Piezoelectric coefficients (e.g., d₃₃):} Quantify the electrical charge generated per unit of applied mechanical force. A high value indicates greater efficiency in energy conversion.

\noindent\textbf{Elastic and dielectric constants:} Define the mechanical response to deformation and the capacity to store electrical energy, respectively. Their optimal combination is crucial for the durability and performance of the device \cite{uchino2010, muralt2008}.

\subsection*{1.5 Operating Mechanism}
At the microscopic level, applied pressure distorts the crystal lattice, displacing positive and negative ions from their equilibrium positions. This charge separation generates an internal electrical polarization, manifested as a measurable voltage across the material's surface electrodes. The magnitude of this voltage is proportional to the applied force and the material's intrinsic properties \cite{muralt2008, jaffe1971}.

\end{paracol}

\section*{2. Piezoelectrics for Electricity Generation}
The ability of piezoelectric materials to convert mechanical deformation into electricity (direct effect) positions them as a key technology for \textbf{energy harvesting}. This principle allows capturing wasted ambient energy, such as vibrations, impacts, or pressure, and transforming it into usable electrical energy to power low-power devices, promoting autonomy and sustainability of electronic systems \cite{roundy2003}.

\subsection*{2.1 Generation Principle and System Configurations}
Piezoelectric energy generation is based on subjecting the material to a source of cyclic mechanical stress. Its performance critically depends on the system design, which adapts to the type of available mechanical source \cite{beeby2006}:

\noindent\textbf{Impact or pressure systems:} Use discs or plates that are compressed intermittently (e.g., piezoelectric floors).

\noindent\textbf{Resonant vibration systems:} Harness constant ambient vibrations (e.g., in industrial machinery or bridges), where the material is tuned to a specific frequency to maximize deformation and, consequently, electrical output.

\noindent\textbf{Flexural systems:} Employ flexible materials like PVDF integrated into surfaces that bend periodically (e.g., in smart footwear or clothing).

\switchcolumn

\subsection*{2.2 Design and Optimization}
The design of an efficient piezoelectric harvester requires optimizing several factors:

\noindent\textbf{Material selection:} As discussed in Section 1, the choice between PZT (high sensitivity) and PVDF (flexibility) is fundamental according to the application.

\noindent\textbf{Geometry and mechanical coupling:} The shape (cantilever, patch, disk) and how force is transferred to the material determine the magnitude of deformation and energy generated.

\noindent\textbf{Circuit conditioning:} The generated electrical signal is typically AC and low voltage. A rectifier circuit, a storage system (supercapacitors or lithium-ion batteries), and a voltage regulator are required to obtain a stable and usable DC output \cite{priya2009}.

\subsection*{2.3 Advantages and Limitations}
This technology offers unique benefits but also presents clear challenges that define its scope of application.

\noindent\textbf{Advantages:} Provides \textbf{energy autonomy} to microsystems, eliminates the need for wiring or frequent battery replacement, has a \textbf{compact design}, and, lacking complex moving parts, is inherently \textbf{robust} with low maintenance \cite{roundy2003, beeby2006}.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations:} Its main restriction is \textbf{low power density} (usually in the order of µW to mW), which limits its use to ultra-low consumption devices. Additionally, its efficiency depends directly on the availability and regularity of the mechanical source, and high-performance ceramic materials (PZT) can be \textbf{fragile} and present fatigue problems \cite{priya2009}.

\subsection*{2.4 Applied Examples}
Despite limitations, successful implementations demonstrate the viability of the concept:

\noindent\textbf{Piezoelectric tiles:} Installed in high pedestrian traffic areas, generating energy for local LED lighting or sensors.

\noindent\textbf{Autonomous structural monitoring:} Small harvesters attached to bridges or machinery capture energy from their operational vibrations to power wireless integrity monitoring sensors, eliminating battery maintenance needs.

\noindent\textbf{Portable devices (Wearables):} Integration into footwear or textiles to power biometric or location sensors, harnessing human movement \cite{beeby2006, roundy2005}.

\end{paracol}

\section*{3. Inherent Merits and Limitations of Piezoelectric Technology}
Beyond its specific application in energy generation (Section 2), piezoelectric technology presents a set of inherent characteristics that define its scope and viability in modern engineering. This analysis contrasts its fundamental capabilities with practical barriers to its implementation.

\subsection*{3.1 Fundamental Merits}
The main strengths of piezoelectric materials lie in their versatility and efficiency:

\noindent\textbf{High Power Density and Fast Response:} They can generate significant voltages (on the order of volts) from minimal deformations (micrometers) and respond to frequencies ranging from less than 1 Hz to the MHz range. This makes them indispensable in applications requiring high precision and speed, such as nanometric positioning actuators, ultrasonic transducers for medical imaging, or impact sensors \cite{erturk2009, nde-transductores}.

\noindent\textbf{Direct Conversion Efficiency:} They convert mechanical energy into electrical energy (and vice versa) directly, without complex intermediate stages. This conversion efficiency is particularly high in ceramic materials like PZT under resonance conditions.

\noindent\textbf{Robustness and Long Service Life:} Lacking traditional mechanical moving parts (such as gears or motors), they exhibit high wear resistance, require minimal maintenance, and can operate continuously for decades in stable environments, making them ideal for embedded or hard-to-access systems \cite{park2019}.

\noindent\textbf{Compatibility with Microsystems and Low-Power Electronics:} Their compact nature and ability to generate useful voltages directly make them highly compatible with modern electronics, especially integrated circuits and IoT (Internet of Things) systems operating in the microwatt to milliwatt range \cite{ti-slyt587}.

\switchcolumn

\subsection*{3.2 Structural and Operational Limitations}
Despite their potential, the technology faces material and physical challenges that condition its design:

\noindent\textbf{Low Output Current and Limited Total Power:} While they can produce high voltages, the generated electrical current is typically low (microamperes). This translates to \textbf{low total power density}, restricting their use to micro-energy applications and ruling them out as a primary source for medium or high-power loads \cite{ahmad2019}.

\noindent\textbf{Critical Dependence on Excitation Source:} Their operation is \textbf{passive and conditional}. They require a continuous external deformation or vibration source to generate electricity usefully. In a static environment, their output is zero, limiting their applicability to scenarios with predictable motion.

\noindent\textbf{Mechanical Fragility (especially in ceramics):} The most efficient materials, such as PZT ceramics, are inherently brittle and susceptible to fracture from impact, cyclic fatigue, or excessive bending stresses, requiring careful design of mechanical coupling and protection \cite{dericioglu2003}.

\noindent\textbf{Environmental Sensitivity:} They have a critical \textbf{Curie temperature}, above which they irreversibly lose their piezoelectric properties. Additionally, some materials (like PZT) may suffer degradation from humidity or intense electric fields, affecting their long-term stability \cite{erturk2009, dericioglu2003}.

\noindent\textbf{Complexity of Conditioning Electronics:} The electrical output signal is AC, irregular, and high impedance. For practical use in standard DC electronic circuits, a conditioning circuit including rectification, regulation, and storage is required, adding complexity, cost, and losses to the complete system \cite{ti-slyt587}.

\subsection*{3.3 Synthesis: A Technological Balance}
Collectively, piezoelectricity offers a unique set of capabilities—speed, precision, durability, and miniaturization—making it optimal for niche sensing, actuation, and micro-generation applications. However, its limitations in power, motion dependence, and fragility impose clear restrictions on its application scope. Successful implementation resides in a design that maximizes its merits within the framework defined by its intrinsic limitations.

\end{paracol}

\section*{4. Examples of Successful Piezoelectric Generation Projects}
The viability of piezoelectric technology for energy harvesting has been validated through pioneering implementations at real scale. These projects demonstrate its adaptability to different contexts—from urban infrastructure to personal devices—and offer valuable lessons on its practical integration.

\subsection*{4.1 Urban and Transport Infrastructure}
Large-scale projects have successfully integrated piezoelectricity into the built environment, harnessing massive energy sources like traffic and pedestrian flow:

\noindent\textbf{Roadway Pavements (Innowattech, Israel):} This company implemented piezoelectric modules embedded under asphalt to capture deformation energy from vehicles. The generated energy, reportedly reaching hundreds of kWh per kilometer under high traffic conditions, is used for street lighting and monitoring systems, demonstrating the potential to create self-sufficient "smart roads" \cite{ottman2012}.

\noindent\textbf{Pedestrian Flooring (Pavegen, UK):} One of the most widely disseminated cases globally. Its triangular tiles convert kinetic energy from footsteps into electricity and data. Installed in airports (Heathrow) and shopping centers, they not only generate energy for local LED lighting (2-5 W per step) but also provide real-time metrics on people flow, integrating energy generation with urban data analytics \cite{pavegen-tech}.

\noindent\textbf{Railway Stations (Japan):} Countries like Japan have been pioneers in installing piezoelectric floors in high-traffic metro stations. These systems harness both pedestrian footsteps and ambient train vibrations to power information displays and sensors, with documented productions on the order of thousands of kWh annually per station, validating their utility in mass transit hubs \cite{beeby2012}.

\switchcolumn

\subsection*{4.2 Portable and Biomedical Devices}
On a smaller but equally significant scale, piezoelectricity has enabled the development of autonomous systems for personal and health applications:

\noindent\textbf{Energy-Harvesting Footwear (University Research):} Various academic and commercial prototypes have integrated flexible materials like PVDF into shoe insoles. Cyclical heel pressure during walking generates between 1 and 5 mW, sufficient to power biometric sensors or location devices, advancing toward battery elimination in wearables \cite{qi2020}.

\noindent\textbf{Self-Powered Pacemaker (ETH Zürich, Switzerland):} This research project represents the technology frontier. It consists of a pacemaker prototype that incorporates a flexible piezoelectric transducer, which converts mechanical energy from heartbeats into electricity to power the device itself. This approach promises to reduce or eliminate battery replacement surgeries, marking a milestone in self-sufficient implantable medical devices \cite{li2013}.

\subsection*{4.3 Autonomous Structural Monitoring}
The robustness and low maintenance of piezoelectrics make them ideal for applications in critical infrastructure where access is difficult:

\noindent\textbf{Bridge Sensors (University of Southampton, UK):} Piezoelectric harvesters have been deployed attached to bridge beams and structural elements. These devices capture energy from traffic- and wind-induced vibrations to power wireless Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) sensor networks. This case is paradigmatic because it solves the fundamental problem of how to permanently power remote sensors, enabling continuous monitoring without battery maintenance \cite{park2019}.

\subsection*{4.4 Lessons Learned and Trends}
Successful projects share common characteristics: they are designed for \textbf{predictable, cyclic mechanical energy sources} (footsteps, traffic, heartbeats), integrate \textbf{efficient conditioning circuits} to handle the low-voltage AC output, and focus on \textbf{ultra-low-power applications}. Collectively, they demonstrate that piezoelectricity is not a solution to replace the electrical grid, but an \textbf{enabling technology} for the energy autonomy of microsystems and the creation of intelligent and sustainable infrastructures and devices.

\end{paracol}

\section*{5. Prototype Proposal: Autonomous Power System for Oceanographic Buoy}
Based on the analysis of piezoelectric technology capabilities and limitations \cite{dericioglu2003, ahmad2019, park2019}, an energy harvesting system integrated into an oceanographic buoy is proposed. The objective is complete energy autonomy for sensors (pH, conductivity, ORP, temperature) and the LoRa communication module, harnessing wave motion.

\subsection*{5.1 General Concept and Mechanical Design}
The prototype consists of an internal generator module transforming buoy rocking motion into electricity. The core is a \textbf{pressurized box} (30 × 20 × 12 cm, 6 mm walls) 3D-printed with \textbf{PETG or reinforced Nylon} for marine corrosion resistance \cite{petg-3d-printing}.

\textbf{Conversion Mechanism:} An internal lever transmits wave-induced torque to 24 PZT-5A piezoelectric discs (27 mm) arranged in 8 series × 3 parallel configuration, optimizing voltage (~160 V peak-to-peak) and current output.

\textbf{Structural Analysis:} Calculations confirm viability:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Torque:} $\tau = F \times r = 25\text{ N} \times 0.12\text{ m} = 3\text{ N·m}$
    \item \textbf{Pressure per disc:} $P = F/A \approx 14\text{ kPa}$
    \item Within PZT-5A optimal range (50–250 kPa) for efficient response and longevity \cite{pzt5a_datasheet, dericioglu2003}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5.2 Electronic Design and Power Management}
The electrical subsystem efficiently conditions, stores, and distributes energy:

\textbf{Signal Conditioning:} AC output rectified and regulated by \textbf{LTC3588-1 IC} for high efficiency with piezoelectric sources \cite{ti-slyt587, ltc3588-datasheet}.

\textbf{Storage:} \textbf{Supercapacitor bank} (0.5F at 11V) preferred over batteries for longer cycle life and rapid charge/discharge \cite{supercapacitor-5_5v}.

\textbf{Workload:} Powers STM32L432 MCU \cite{stm32l432-datasheet}, LoRa RFM95W module \cite{rfm95w-datasheet}, and industrial sensors \cite{atlas-scientific-sensors}.

\subsection*{5.3 Performance Estimation and Viability}
Quantitative analysis contrasts energy generation with requirements (ver Tabla \ref{tab:power_budget} for detailed power budget):

\textbf{System Demand:} Total consumption \textbf{∼2.16 mAh/day} dominated by brief sensor activation and data transmission periods.

\textbf{System Generation:} Conservative estimates (0.4 Hz average wave action) yield substantial surplus: \textbf{∼1400 mAh/day after conversion losses}.

\textbf{Viability Conclusion:} Generation exceeds consumption by >600×, guaranteeing continuous operation with robust safety margin for calm conditions or additional sensors.

\subsection*{5.4 Technical Specifications}
Key components and specifications are summarized in Tabla \ref{tab:specifications}. The system employs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Piezoelectric:} 24× PZT-5A discs, 27 mm (high sensitivity d₃₃) \cite{pzt5a_datasheet, murata-pzt, tdk-piezo}
    \item \textbf{Circuit:} LTC3588-1 with Schottky rectifier \cite{ltc3588-datasheet}
    \item \textbf{Storage:} Supercapacitor bank 0.5F @ 11V \cite{supercapacitor-5_5v}
    \item \textbf{MCU:} STM32L432KC (<2 µA in deep sleep) \cite{stm32l432-datasheet}
    \item \textbf{Communication:} LoRa RFM95W (915 MHz) \cite{rfm95w-datasheet}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data Transmission:} Hourly packets follow format in Tabla \ref{tab:data_format}, including sensor measurements and supercapacitor charge level.

\subsection*{5.5 Final Conclusions and General Recommendations}
\textbf{Final Conclusions:}
This integrated design demonstrates practical, efficient piezoelectric generation for remote monitoring \cite{roundy2003, beeby2006, park2019}. The proposal synthesizes previous analyses—material selection (PZT) \cite{safari2008, uchino2010, muralt2008}, system design, electronic conditioning—into a quantitatively justified solution. It represents a viable step toward sustainable, autonomous oceanographic sensing.

\textbf{General Recommendations:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation:} Test in wave tank with controlled frequencies (0.1–1.0 Hz)
    \item \textbf{Components:} Use PZT-5A discs from Murata \cite{murata-pzt} or TDK \cite{tdk-piezo}, LTC3588-1 circuit \cite{ltc3588-datasheet}
    \item \textbf{Sensors:} Atlas Scientific Industrial series for reliability \cite{atlas-scientific-sensors}
    \item \textbf{Field Testing:} Deploy prototype in controlled marine environment
    \item \textbf{Future Work:} Implement machine learning for adaptive power management
    \item \textbf{Scalability:} Design for multi-buoy network deployment
\end{itemize}
